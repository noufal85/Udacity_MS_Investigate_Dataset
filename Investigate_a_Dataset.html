Appointment no-shows in Brazil¶

Introduction¶

Why are some medical appointments missed? This report looks at how missed appointments differ by patient age, the number of days between booking and the appointment, and whether a text message was recorded.

The data contains 110,527 appointments in Brazil, dated April 29 to June 8, 2016. Each row is one appointment, so a patient can appear more than once. The results describe patterns in these records; they do not tell us what caused a patient to miss an appointment.

Questions¶

The analysis follows four questions. Each comparison looks at the percentage of appointments missed, rather than just the number missed.

  1. Which age groups missed a larger share of their appointments?
  2. Were appointments booked further in advance missed more often?
  3. How did missed appointments differ between records with and without a text message?
  4. Does the message comparison change when appointments have similar booking delays? How do age and booking delay look together?

Data description¶

This section explains the columns used in the report. A text message is called an SMS in the dataset and in the chart labels.

The attendance column needs care: No-show = Yes means the patient missed the appointment; No means the patient attended. A no-show rate of 20% means that 20 out of every 100 appointments were missed.

Cleaning decisions¶

The checks found no missing values or duplicate appointments. Two kinds of record could not be used in the comparisons:

  • One record had a negative age.
  • Five records put the appointment date before the booking date.

These six records are removed. Missing values are not filled in, and no other appointments are removed. The count table below shows the effect of each step.

Measuring the booking delay¶

Booking delay means the number of calendar days from booking to the appointment. If both happened on the same date, the delay is zero.

The appointment's time of day is not available. Comparing dates, rather than exact hours, avoids treating an afternoon booking as later than a same-day appointment stored with a midnight timestamp.

Keeping age and repeat visits¶

Age zero is kept because the data includes infants. High ages, including 115, are also kept because there is no supporting information to correct them.

A patient can have several real appointments. Those visits are kept because this report counts appointments, not people. Column names are shortened for readability; the recorded disability levels are kept as supplied.

def clean_appointments(raw):
    """Return one row per usable appointment and sequential exclusion counts."""
    required = {"PatientId", "AppointmentID", "Age", "ScheduledDay",
                "AppointmentDay", "SMS_received", "No-show"}
    if not required.issubset(raw.columns):
        raise ValueError(f"Missing required columns: {sorted(required - set(raw.columns))}")
    labels = set(raw["No-show"].dropna().unique())
    if not labels.issubset({"Yes", "No"}):
        raise ValueError(f"Unexpected No-show labels: {labels}")

    df = raw.rename(columns=SOURCE_COLUMNS).copy()
    audit = [{"rule": "Input appointments", "removed": 0, "remaining": len(df)}]

    def exclude(mask, reason):
        nonlocal df
        removed = int(mask.sum())
        df = df.loc[~mask].copy()
        audit.append({"rule": reason, "removed": removed, "remaining": len(df)})

    exclude(df.duplicated(), "Exact duplicate rows")
    if df["appointment_id"].duplicated().any():
        raise ValueError("Conflicting duplicate appointment IDs require investigation")
    for column in ["scheduled_day", "appointment_day"]:
        df[column] = pd.to_datetime(df[column], utc=True, errors="coerce")
    df["age"] = pd.to_numeric(df["age"], errors="coerce")
    df["sms_received"] = pd.to_numeric(df["sms_received"], errors="coerce")
    critical = ["patient_id", "appointment_id", "age", "scheduled_day",
                "appointment_day", "sms_received", "no_show_label"]
    exclude(df[critical].isna().any(axis=1), "Missing or unparseable critical fields")
    if not df["sms_received"].isin([0, 1]).all():
        raise ValueError("Unexpected SMS_received values")
    exclude(df["age"] < 0, "Negative ages")

    # Appointment timestamps omit appointment time; compare encoded calendar dates.
    df["waiting_days"] = (
        df["appointment_day"].dt.normalize() - df["scheduled_day"].dt.normalize()
    ).dt.days
    exclude(df["waiting_days"] < 0, "Appointment precedes scheduling date")
    df["no_show"] = df["no_show_label"].eq("Yes")
    df["age_band"] = pd.cut(
        df["age"], [0, 18, 35, 50, 65, np.inf], right=False,
        labels=["Under 18", "18-34", "35-49", "50-64", "65+"],
    )
    df["waiting_band"] = pd.cut(
        df["waiting_days"], [-1, 0, 7, 14, 30, 60, np.inf],
        labels=["Same day", "1-7 days", "8-14 days", "15-30 days", "31-60 days", "61+ days"],
    )
    df["sms_group"] = pd.Categorical(
        df["sms_received"].map({0: "No SMS recorded", 1: "SMS recorded"}),
        categories=["No SMS recorded", "SMS recorded"], ordered=True,
    )
    return df.reset_index(drop=True), pd.DataFrame(audit)


def group_rates(df, columns):
    """Keep denominators alongside observed group rates; empty groups stay absent."""
    result = df.groupby(columns, observed=True, dropna=False)["no_show"].agg(
        appointments="size", no_shows="sum", no_show_rate="mean",
    )
    return result.assign(no_show_pct=lambda x: 100 * x["no_show_rate"])


def label_plot(ax, title, xlabel, ylabel):
    ax.set(title=title, xlabel=xlabel, ylabel=ylabel)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
    ax.grid(axis="y", alpha=0.2)
    return ax

    columns = {'appointments': 'Appointments', 'no_shows': 'Missed appointments',

    display(table[list(columns)].rename(columns=columns).round(2))

Data used in the analysis¶

The cleaning leaves 110,521 appointments for 62,298 patients. Of these appointments, 22,314 were missed (20.19%).

That is about one missed appointment in every five. This overall percentage is the starting point for the age, booking-delay and message comparisons below.

What the booking-delay comparison shows¶

Only 4.65% of same-day appointments were missed. This compares with 24.15% for appointments booked 1–7 days ahead and 34.15% for those booked 31–60 days ahead.

The rate then falls to 28.45% for delays of 61 days or more. It does not keep rising for every longer delay. These groups may include different patients and appointment types, so the results do not prove that shortening a delay would prevent a missed appointment.

What changes when booking delay is considered¶

In all five delay ranges where both SMS groups appear, the SMS-recorded group had a lower missed-appointment rate. This is the opposite of the overall comparison. Same-day appointments help explain the difference: they had a low missed-appointment rate, and none had an SMS recorded.

The message groups are still not identical. For example, they may differ in age or appointment type, even within the same delay range. These results show why comparing only the overall percentages can be misleading; they do not tell us what the messages caused.

Conclusions¶

This section brings the answers to the four questions together. Of the 110,521 appointments used in the analysis, 22,314 were missed (20.19%)—about one in five.

Age¶

People aged 18–34 missed a larger share of their appointments (23.97%) than those aged 65 and above (15.50%). The difference does not tell us why a particular appointment was missed.

Booking delay¶

Same-day appointments were missed much less often (4.65%) than appointments booked 31–60 days ahead (34.15%). The rate did not keep rising for appointments booked furthest ahead.

Text messages¶

Overall, the SMS-recorded group had a higher missed-appointment rate. But within every booking-delay range where both groups appeared, it had a lower rate. The overall numbers alone would therefore give an incomplete picture.

Taken together, these findings show why age, booking delay and message records should be considered together when discussing missed appointments. They do not establish that age, a longer delay or a message caused a missed visit.

Limitations¶

The records can show patterns, but they cannot answer every question about missed appointments. The following limits matter when using the findings.

Messages were not assigned at random¶

Patients with and without messages may have differed before any message was sent. The records also do not say when messages arrived or whether they were read. A difference between these groups is not enough to show that reminders helped or harmed attendance.

Appointments are not the same as patients¶

The 110,521 appointments came from 62,298 patients. A person with several appointments counts several times. The percentages describe missed appointments, not the percentage of people who missed a visit.

The data covers a short period¶

These records cover April 29 to June 8, 2016, in the Brazilian appointment data supplied for the project. The patterns may differ in other places or today. A longer period would help show whether they stay similar over time.

Important details are missing¶

The data does not explain travel difficulties, work commitments, appointment purpose or why a visit was rescheduled. It also gives appointment dates without times of day. These details could help explain missed appointments, and grouping ages or delays into ranges cannot account for all of them.

What to investigate next¶

A next study could record when reminders were sent, the type of appointment, and cancellations or rescheduling. It could also look at each patient's history rather than treating every visit as a separate comparison.

To find out whether reminders improve attendance, a study would need a fair way to decide which appointments receive them. That question should be answered before using these results to change reminder policies.
